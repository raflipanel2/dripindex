FIELD GUIDE 12 / OUTDOOR

Irrigation audit: observe the landscape before adding minutes

Use runoff, soil conditions and local rules to decide whether outdoor watering is excessive.

By DripIndex EditorialPublished & reviewed 11 Nov 2026Educational field note

A green lawn is not proof that an irrigation schedule is efficient, and brown spots do not always mean the sprinklers need more minutes. Soil, shade, slope, weather and nozzle coverage all shape results. This guide builds a record of what the system does rather than recommending universal runtime schedules that may be wrong for your climate.

What to notice first

Before changing equipment, record the timing and conditions of the observation. These are clues worth investigating, not proof of a particular fault.

  • Water runs onto sidewalks or driveways during a sprinkler cycle.
  • One zone leaves dry patches beside overwatered sections.
  • The controller operates after rainfall or during times prohibited by local rules.

A controlled check

Use the same setting, timing and measurement method whenever possible. Stop if a test would require unsafe access, pressurized disassembly or exposure to electricity.

  1. 01

    Write down current start times, zone durations and the day-of-week schedule before making changes. Take a photo of the controller display.

  2. 02

    Observe a normal cycle from a safe place. Map overspray, runoff, blocked heads, puddles and unusually dry strips.

  3. 03

    Check weather and local utility watering restrictions; adjust only within the controller manual and the site’s irrigation requirements.

  4. 04

    Reassess after several cycles. If there are broken heads, underground leaks or electrical controller issues, involve an irrigation professional.

How to interpret what you find

Runoff may reflect compacted soil or excessive application speed rather than simply “too many minutes”. A low area that stays wet may be collecting water from elsewhere. EPA WaterSense outdoor guidance discusses matching watering to conditions and using appropriate controllers; its advice should be adapted to local climate and rules.

EXAMPLE / FIELD RECORD

Field note: zone 3 runs 15 minutes; runoff starts near minute 7 on the driveway slope while the shaded bed still looks wet the next morning. That observation supports a targeted adjustment or professional coverage review, not increasing every zone.

What to check next

To compare two schedule changes fairly, keep the weather and measured soil conditions in view. A week of heavy rain can make a reduced irrigation cycle appear more effective than it really is. Likewise, a dry windy week may increase apparent need without indicating malfunction. Record the cycle date, weather, visible runoff and any controller adjustments. Then ask whether the desired plant outcome was achieved with less overspray. An irrigation professional may need to assess soil and nozzle distribution.

Pitfalls and safety boundaries

Do not assume plant needs, irrigation rules or drought restrictions are identical everywhere. Hidden electrical faults and pressurized underground pipes are professional work.

If the source is hidden, a fixture is failing, or moisture continues, contact a qualified plumber or your property manager. Protect occupants from slip, electrical and contamination risks. This article cannot inspect your individual plumbing system.

Primary reference and verification
