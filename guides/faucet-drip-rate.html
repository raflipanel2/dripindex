FIELD GUIDE 03 / LEAK DETECTION

Measure a faucet drip before estimating water waste

Count the drops, state the assumed drop size, and understand why estimates vary.

By DripIndex EditorialPublished & reviewed 11 Nov 2026Educational field note

A slow dripping faucet feels negligible because each drop is tiny, yet a sustained drip can add up. The challenge is that there is no universal faucet-drop volume. The USGS drip calculator uses one quarter of a milliliter per drop as an illustrative assumption. Recording the actual drop rate makes the estimate more useful than guessing from a photograph or applying a fixed annual loss.

What to notice first

Before changing equipment, record the timing and conditions of the observation. These are clues worth investigating, not proof of a particular fault.

  • Individual drops fall when the faucet is fully off.
  • The drip changes with time of day or pressure.
  • A dry basin develops persistent wet spots without anyone using the tap.

A controlled check

Use the same setting, timing and measurement method whenever possible. Stop if a test would require unsafe access, pressurized disassembly or exposure to electricity.

  1. 01

    Put a visible timer next to the faucet. Count drops for a full minute; for very slow drips, count over five minutes and divide by five.

  2. 02

    Repeat the count twice at comparable times. If the rate swings widely, report a range instead of one exact value.

  3. 03

    Enter the average drops per minute in the Drip Loss Calculator. Keep the default 0.25 mL drop size only as an assumption, not a precise measurement.

  4. 04

    Take a photo of the fixture model if known and note whether the handle closes fully. If you cannot identify or safely isolate the fixture, ask maintenance or a plumber to inspect it.

How to interpret what you find

At 12 drops per minute and an assumed 0.25 mL each, the estimate is 4.32 liters in a day, about 129.6 liters in 30 days. Real droplet sizes differ with aerator, surface tension and faucet shape; that is why the calculator lets you change the assumed volume. This estimate is a planning aid and will not equal an exact meter reading.

EXAMPLE / FIELD RECORD

Field note: kitchen faucet, 11, 13 and 12 drops per minute across three one-minute counts; average 12. The handle is fully closed and the drip persists. The action is to schedule inspection rather than assuming another fixture created the loss.

What to check next

If the dripping stops after a few moments, do not extrapolate the first minute to an entire day. Some taps release residual water from the spout after closing; a persistent repeating drip from a fully shut fixture is a different observation. Start a timer five minutes after use, count again, and compare. When the rate varies with pressure or temperature, store the lower and upper counts and report a range. The loss calculator estimates a sustained rate, so explain when that assumption does not hold.

Pitfalls and safety boundaries

A tiny drip is not the same as a high-volume concealed leak; prioritize pooled water, electrical hazards, and actively flowing leaks. Never force a seized valve to stop a drip.

If the source is hidden, a fixture is failing, or moisture continues, contact a qualified plumber or your property manager. Protect occupants from slip, electrical and contamination risks. This article cannot inspect your individual plumbing system.
