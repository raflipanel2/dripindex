FIELD GUIDE 01 / LEAK DETECTION

The no-use water meter test: how to spot a hidden leak

Separate a possible plumbing leak from normal household water use with a controlled observation.

By DripIndex EditorialPublished & reviewed 11 Nov 2026Educational field note

A water meter that moves while every fixture is idle is a useful clue, but not a diagnosis. Refrigerators, water softeners, irrigation timers and household members can create legitimate use during a test. A good meter check starts by controlling those variables and recording exactly what changed. This guide gives you a repeatable protocol rather than a claim that any moving dial means a broken pipe.

What to notice first

Before changing equipment, record the timing and conditions of the observation. These are clues worth investigating, not proof of a particular fault.

  • The bill has risen even though the household routine feels unchanged; first compare similar billing periods.
  • You can hear filling or running water when nobody is using a tap; a toilet may be cycling quietly.
  • A meter indicator moves repeatedly during a period when all intentional uses should be off.

A controlled check

Use the same setting, timing and measurement method whenever possible. Stop if a test would require unsafe access, pressurized disassembly or exposure to electricity.

  1. 01

    Find the actual meter and identify its units before taking notes. Some meters use cubic feet, others cubic meters or gallons; photograph the full display with the time.

  2. 02

    Ask everyone in the home to avoid faucets, toilets, laundry and showers during the test window. Suspend automatic irrigation or appliances that deliberately refill if you can do so safely.

  3. 03

    Observe the meter for a short interval, then again after a longer no-use window of up to two hours. Record the start and end readings rather than relying only on a spinning indicator.

  4. 04

    If the reading changes, check the most common silent uses—especially toilets—before assuming underground or in-wall leakage. Repeat under comparable conditions and contact a qualified plumber if evidence persists.

How to interpret what you find

An unchanged reading during a controlled interval is reassuring for that interval, not proof that intermittent leaks cannot occur. A change is evidence of flow somewhere past the meter, but the culprit could be a toilet refill or automated device rather than damaged piping. If the meter is shared among units, this test may not isolate your household. Check with the utility about meter features and what area it measures.

EXAMPLE / FIELD RECORD

Field note: 8:00 AM, reading 146.328 m³. At 10:00 AM, reading 146.343 m³ with every planned fixture idle. The difference is 0.015 m³, or 15 liters. That is worth investigating, not an automatic estimate of every future day. Next test: isolate the toilet check and repeat with the same household conditions.

What to check next

If a meter moves only on some days, examine the household schedule before extending the test indefinitely. A softener regeneration cycle, automated garden controller or appliance refill may happen at a fixed interval and will be missed during a short check. Note the day and time, then repeat at the same hour with those legitimate uses accounted for. When asking a utility for help, provide the two readings, their units and the conditions; a technician can then advise what the meter actually monitors.

Pitfalls and safety boundaries

Do not close valves you cannot identify, disconnect a meter, disturb seals, or assume a digital display is broken because its least significant digit changes. If water is near electrics, leave the area and use qualified help.

If the source is hidden, a fixture is failing, or moisture continues, contact a qualified plumber or your property manager. Protect occupants from slip, electrical and contamination risks. This article cannot inspect your individual plumbing system.
